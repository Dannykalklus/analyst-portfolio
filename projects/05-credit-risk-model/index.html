Credit default risk model

Which cardholders will miss next month's payment? Two models trained on 30,000 real credit card accounts (UCI, Yeh & Lien 2009). The scores are then turned into a decline strategy a lender could use.

ROC curves

True positive rate vs false positive rate on the held-out test set

Gradient boosting beats logistic regression by 0.032 AUC. Five-fold cross-validation gives the same ranking (0.787 vs 0.762), so the gap is not luck of the split.

Bad rate by risk decile

Gradient boosting; decile 1 = riskiest 10% of accounts

Default rates fall from 69.7% in the riskiest decile to 3.8% in the safest. Predicted and actual rates track closely, so the scores can be read as probabilities.

Repayment status drives default

Default rate by last month's repayment status (raw data, all 30,000 accounts)

Status −2 to 0 means no balance, paid in full or paid on time; 1 to 4 means months overdue. One month late almost triples the default rate, and two months late pushes it to 69.1%.

What the logistic model weights

Standardised coefficients; positive raises default risk

Repayment behaviour dominates. Higher payments and higher credit limits lower risk. Sex and marital status were left out of the model on fair-lending grounds.

Logistic regression is easier to explain to a credit committee and regulator. Gradient boosting ranks risk better. A common approach is to keep the logistic model as the official scorecard and use the boosted model as a challenger.

Method

Features engineered from six months of history: months paid late, worst delay, latest repayment status, utilisation (current and average), latest payment-to-bill ratio, average payment, bill trend, credit limit, age and education. 70/30 stratified split (seed 42), five-fold cross-validation on the training set, and evaluation on the untouched test set using AUC, Gini, KS and Brier score.

Limitation: the data are Taiwanese cardholders from 2005, so the model shows the method. It is not ready to deploy on a Ghanaian lender's book without retraining on local data.

Data: Yeh, I.-C. & Lien, C. (2009), "The comparisons of data mining techniques for the predictive accuracy of probability of default of credit card clients", Expert Systems with Applications 36(2), 2473-2480; UCI Machine Learning Repository dataset 350. Python (pandas, scikit-learn) and Chart.js. Analysis by Daniel N. T. Tackie.
